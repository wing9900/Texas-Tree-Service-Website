## How far is Conroe Tree Co. from The Woodlands?

About fifteen minutes. Our yard sits north of The Woodlands in Conroe, and crews run straight down I-45 to any of the nine villages. That covers Grogan's Mill, Panther Creek, Cochran's Crossing, Indian Springs, Alden Bridge, Sterling Ridge, College Park, Creekside Park, and Carlton Woods, plus the commercial corridors around Hughes Landing, Market Street, and the Cynthia Woods Mitchell Pavilion.

Short drive time is not a small detail here. When a pine comes down across a driveway on Research Forest Drive at nine at night, the question is who can get a bucket truck there before midnight.

## Do you need approval before removing a tree in The Woodlands?

In most of The Woodlands, yes. The Township and the village review committees require written approval before a live tree of roughly six inches in trunk diameter or larger is removed, and trees on Township reserves cannot be taken at all. The rule exists because the community was planted inside an existing loblolly pine forest in 1974 and the canopy is written into the covenants.

We deal with this every week. Before a saw comes out we document the tree's condition, photograph the defect, and submit what the committee needs. If the tree is dead, hazardous, or beetle infested, that documentation is usually what moves the approval along. You can read the Township's own rules on the [permitting process](https://www.thewoodlandstownship-tx.gov/Departments/Covenant-Administration/Permitting-Process) before you call.

If the tree is leaning over a roof and the answer cannot wait, that is [hazardous tree removal](/services/tree-removal/hazardous-tree-removal/), and the standard changes.

## Which trees fail most often in The Woodlands?

Loblolly pine, by a wide margin. The original forest here is roughly the same age across whole villages, which means large numbers of pines hit their decline years at once. A mature loblolly in Grogan's Mill or Panther Creek can stand eighty feet or more with almost all of its weight in the top third, and it is usually standing over a house.

Water oak is the second problem. It grows fast, holds a lot of canopy, and drops limbs in wind well before it looks unhealthy. Sweetgum and southern red oak round out most of what we take down in the older villages.

The failure pattern is consistent. Drought weakens a pine, Ips engraver beetles or southern pine beetle move into the stressed wood, and the crown browns from the top down over a few weeks. Once the needles are red the tree is gone. Caught earlier, [insect and borer treatment](/services/tree-health/insect-borer-treatment/) can save the trees around it. The Texas A&M Forest Service publishes [current pine bark beetle guidance](https://tfsweb.tamu.edu/) worth reading if you have a stand of pines.

## Can a big pine be saved instead of removed?

Often, yes. A structurally sound pine with a single weak union does not need to come down. [Cabling and bracing](/services/tree-health/cabling-bracing/) supports the union mechanically, and [crown reduction](/services/tree-trimming/crown-reduction/) takes weight and sail area off the top without topping the tree.

That matters more in The Woodlands than anywhere else we work, because removal here costs you a permit, a replanting obligation in some villages, and a gap in a canopy your property value is partly built on.

## What does a Woodlands yard look like after a hurricane?

Predictable, which helps. The May 2024 derecho and Hurricane Beryl both ran through this corridor and did the same thing: snapped pines at mid trunk, rolled shallow rooted water oaks out of saturated ground, and dropped limbs across driveways and power drops.

Two things shorten that week. First, [storm preparation trimming](/services/tree-trimming/storm-preparation-trimming/) before the season, which thins sail area on the trees closest to the house. Second, knowing who to call at six in the morning. Our emergency line runs around the clock, and in The Woodlands that means a crew on site while the roads are still being cleared.
